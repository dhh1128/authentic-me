# Why bother?

To some extent, I feel like this is a bit of a vanity project. The world mostly doesn't care what's authentically Daniel and what's not &mdash; and it shouldn't have to. As G. K. Chesterton observed so insightfully:

>“How much larger your life would be if your self could become smaller in it; if you could really look at other men with common curiosity and pleasure; if you could see them walking as they are in their sunny selfishness and their virile indifference! You would begin to be interested in them, because they are not interested in you. You would break out of this tiny and tawdry theatre in which your own little plot is always played, and you would find yourself under a freer sky, in a street full of splendid strangers.” &mdash; <cite>Orthodoxy</cite> (Garden City, New York: Image Books, 1959), pp. 20–21.

And yet... 

I have been concerned for a long time with the insidious effects of lying and fraud in our society and in our personal lives. I've referenced the topic in numerous public conferences. In 2017, I published an [essay](https://sivanea.com/2017/10/22/on-lying-and-truth-telling/) on the subject. In 2023, I wrote an [open letter](https://dhh1128.github.io/papers/ctf.pdf) to warn that new innovations in AI were going to make the problem far, far worse.

I work in cybersecurity and identity technology. This industry offers important insights and useful techniques to fight the problem. However, they're not accessible to ordinary people who just want to get on with their lives, or to those who may have less access to education or technology. Plus, entrenched interests in markets and governments have strong incentives to delay adoption, or to co-opt the features &mdash; even as they trumpet their enthusiasm.

This has produced an odd dynamic: *I've worked for years on authentic identity, but I myself have not had one, because I've been waiting for a system to come along that gives it to me easily*. And I've waited and waited.

Which brings me to now.

I'm tired of waiting for adoption, and I've realized that my waiting has been, in and of itself, a symptom of dysfunction. Self-sovereign identity is supposed to be something that individuals actively claim. Waiting for a system to give it to me is inconsistent with this ideal. If I really want independence, I shouldn't expect someone else or someone else's system to give it to me.

That doesn't mean I won't use tech. It just means that my identity will be *facilitated by* tech rather than *derivative of* or *captive to* it &mdash; and the [tech I use](tech.md) will be my own choice, not someone else's "system". It also means that I'm setting the bar very low: no new tech should have to be invented or adopted, and no new spec or standard should have to be adopted, to decide if it's really me.

In some ways, this is an experiment: me living my idealistic vision to see how it goes. I believe that, if questions about the authenticity of my identity are evaluated according to what I publish here, it should be possible to prevent impersonation by almost any kind of fraudster, whether human or AI, even under very determined attack. So let's see if I can pull it off...

I don't pretend that what I'm doing here &mdash; or what must be done by someone who wants to verify me &mdash; is practical for the average person right now. I've heard a critique that the ideals of SSI are a libertarian thought exercise for spoiled, tech-savvy, navel-gazing intellectuals living in permissive democracies. Speaking just about myself, maybe there's a grain of truth in this, sometimes. :-) But I don't think that's the *whole* truth about these ideals, or even the *most important* truth about them. And I think it's high time to learn by doing *myself* instead of by conferencing and prototyping and pitching and standardizing "systems" driven by market forces.

It's time for me to learn to drive.
